01 — About me

About me

I am an atmospheric scientist with a particular interest in tropospheric gas-phase photochemistry and the role of nitrogen oxides (NOx) and ozone (O3). My research investigates how photochemical processes and emissions shape atmospheric composition, with important implications for the oxidizing capacity of the troposphere and the lifetime of greenhouse gases. My work spans a wide range of spatial scales and environments: from local air quality to photochemical processes in the remote upper troposphere.

I completed my PhD at the Max Planck Institute for Chemistry in Mainz under the supervision of Dr. Horst Fischer and Prof. Jos Lelieveld. My background is in experimental atmospheric chemistry and airborne measurements of nitrogen oxides. Since October 2024, I have been a postdoctoral researcher in Prof. Colette Heald’s group at ETH Zürich, where I have expanded my work to include global atmospheric modelling. In October 2026, I will begin a two-year Marie Skłodowska-Curie Postdoctoral Fellowship at ETH Zürich. My project, SHOTGAP, will investigate the processes driving increases in tropospheric ozone across the Southern Hemisphere.

02 — My research

My research

Below is a selection of research projects I have worked on. For a complete overview of my work, please see my publications.

Air quality in Switzerland: Hot days - unhealthy air

Ground-level ozone is a harmful air pollutant associated with adverse health effects and premature mortality. We show that ozone levels in Switzerland are particularly high on hot, sunny days. Although substantial reductions in precursor emissions have improved overall ozone air quality, rising temperatures over recent decades have offset some of these benefits—a phenomenon known as the climate penalty (Nussbaumer et al., 2026).

Aircraft measurements in the tropical troposphere

I led the nitrogen oxide (NOx) measurements during the Chemistry of the Atmosphere Field Experiment campaigns in Brazil (CAFE Brazil, 2022/23) and the Pacific (CAFE Pacific, 2024) aboard the German research aircraft HALO.

CAFE Brazil. Using observations collected over the Amazon, we showed that NOx from lightning controls the mechanism of ozone formation in the upper tropical troposphere (Nussbaumer et al., 2024).

CAFE Pacific. Measurements over the Indo-Pacific Warm Pool allowed us to trace exceptionally low ozone to deep convection occurring in the absence of lightning (Nussbaumer et al., 2025).

NOx emissions from Los Angeles

During a research stay at UC Berkeley as part of my PhD, I studied NOx emissions across Los Angeles using airborne observations from the 2021 RECAP-CA (Re-Evaluating the Chemistry of Air Pollutants in California) campaign. By combining measurements of NOx and winds, we derived spatially resolved emission estimates that can help evaluate and improve local emission inventories (Nussbaumer et al., 2023).
